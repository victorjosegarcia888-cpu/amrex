# 1. Structural architecture: why it does not delaminate

## 2D laminate versus 3D interlock weave

In a conventional laminate, the layers are stacked in two dimensions and the properties along the thickness direction depend on the residual matrix between layers. Under severe thermal and mechanical loads, interlaminar shear stresses appear and promote cracking and catastrophic separation of the material.

In contrast, a 3D interlock fiber preform eliminates the weak plane. The yarns continue in several directions and, most importantly, traverse the thickness. This creates structural continuity that prevents a crack from propagating as a free delamination.

## Fiber bridging mechanism

When a thermal crack tries to advance, it encounters fibers oriented along the Z axis and in other directions. The crack cannot open cleanly because the fibers act as bridges. Energy is dissipated through friction, microscopic pull-out, and stress redistribution.

This behavior is crucial for avoiding failure of the “layer peeling” type. Instead, the material responds with a more gradual and controlled degradation.

## Relationship with quasicrystal architecture

Quasicrystals offer a geometry that is not based on simple periodicity. This is interesting from a structural point of view because:

- it avoids repetitive failure patterns;
- it disperses stresses in multiple directions;
- it reduces the chance of localized concentrations;
- it creates a more complex connection network than a classical periodic lattice.

In this sense, quasicrystal design can be seen as a geometric inspiration for a 3D interlock architecture resistant to delamination.
